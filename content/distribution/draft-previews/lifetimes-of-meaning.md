# Lifetimes of Meaning

## What AI Music Is Missing

I've been working with someone in the music industry on AI research and development. The kind of work where you push the tools hard enough to find the edges. Recently, I pushed one of the newest music generation models to see what those edges actually feel like.

---

## The First Listen

The first thing I did was ask it to create a track in the style of my own band. Progressive metal from Brooklyn, early 2010s. Odd time signatures, layered guitars, atmospheric density. Our videos are all over YouTube. I figured there was plenty of training data.

It didn't sound anything like my band. It sounded like generic music in the genre — slightly overproduced for the kind of underground vibe you'd expect from a progressive band in Brooklyn, more like weirder pop metal with odd time signatures and no singer. The sound quality was sharp. Lira 3 came out crisp and well-mixed. But it anchored to the prompt and the genre label, not to anything specific about us. It filled in the rest with averages.

---

## The Soulless Solo

The track was rolling along reasonably well, and then it started soloing. Not badly. Not out of key. But in this aimless, decorative way that felt like someone noodling at a jazz jam session. Just running through shapes. Technically present, emotionally absent.

This is the thing that's hard to explain to someone who hasn't sat in a room with musicians. A real solo isn't a sequence of correct notes. It's a conversation with everything that came before it in the song. The soloist is reacting to the rhythm section, to the harmonic tension that's been building, to the thing the vocalist just said. The notes they choose carry the weight of every note they decided not to play.

The AI solo had none of that. It had access to every note that's ever been played in the genre, and it chose a perfectly average selection of them. The lottery ball machine picked reasonable numbers. Nothing was wrong. Nothing was alive.

---

## Where It Works

Here's what surprised me: the AI actually does rap well.

I wasn't expecting that. But when I generated hip-hop tracks, something clicked. The ensemble of voices, the rhythmic layering, the way the beat carries the structure. It worked. The AI connected the pieces in a way that felt intentional, or at least coherent enough that the seams didn't show.

I think the reason is structural. Rap has a tighter relationship between rhythm and meaning. The beat is a grid. The flow maps to the grid. The words carry rhythm as much as content. There's less room for the AI to wander into the kind of aimless noodling that killed the metal solo. The constraints of the form do work that the AI can't do for itself.

That observation connects back to what I learned building the software factory. Structured prompts beat verbose ones because they remove the space where models drift. In music, structured genres seem to do the same thing. Give the AI a grid, and it stays on it. Give it freedom, and it picks the lottery.

---

## The Inspiration Problem

The AI music that produces full tracks feels like its best use case is as inspiration for real human work. Or background music. Those are both legitimate. But neither is what artists actually want.

The problem is this: when the AI generates a good idea, it generates the whole track. It's already done. The idea is trapped inside a finished product you didn't make.

What's missing is the ability to reach into the generation and pull out the thing that was interesting. Maybe it's a chord progression that appeared at the 45-second mark. Maybe it's a rhythmic pattern in the verse. Maybe it's one specific textural choice. The good idea is in there, but you can't extract it. You can't take that fragment and transplant it into your own work, reshape it, build on it, make it yours.

Right now, AI music generation gives you simulations of what could be. Full-length demonstrations of a possible direction. That's useful. But we need tools that let you treat those simulations as raw material, not final product. Take the good sections. Transfer the theme into another piece. Use the generation as a sketch, not a painting.

Those tools don't exist yet.

---

## The Ping Pong Ball

The AI has access to every idea. Every chord, every rhythm, every production technique across every genre that exists in its training data. And it connects them the way a ping pong ball connects with lottery numbers. The selections are reasonable. They're statistically defensible. They sound like the genre they're supposed to sound like.

But that's exactly the problem.

Pattern matching in a good human artist doesn't gravitate toward what's statistically reasonable. It gravitates toward what feels different. What's exciting. What's interesting. The human filter isn't random and it isn't average. It's shaped by a lifetime of listening to music, of hearing things that moved you and things that didn't, of developing taste that's as specific as a fingerprint.

The real test has always been this: the human listens to sounds and thinks they're awesome, then puts them together. It's the filter of that person's lifetime of experience and how they relate to it. That filter is different for every human who's ever made music. That's why music made by different people sounds different, even when they're working in the same genre with the same tools.

The AI doesn't have a lifetime. It has a dataset. Those are not the same thing.

---

## The Bridge

This leads somewhere uncomfortable for artists.

As AI begins to replace more of what humans used to create themselves, where does the role of the human stay? Not where does it go eventually, but where does it stay right now, during this transition?

I see the value in giving humans more advanced tools to generate better music using AI's well-produced ideas as a starting point. The quality out of the box is genuinely impressive. But the human needs a way to move the notes around. Change things specifically. Take ownership of the composition through acts of deliberate choice.

Without that, AI music has a band-in-a-box feeling. You pressed a button and something came out. It's fine to have that experience if you want it. There's nothing wrong with generating background music or exploring what a genre sounds like. But the reason real artists are upset about it is because it cheapens the art form to have it made so simply. The complexity of the process is part of what gives the output meaning. Not because difficulty is virtuous, but because the choices a skilled person makes under constraints are what separate one piece of music from every other piece of music.

The tools to make music in this more advanced way don't exist yet. We're in a bridge period. Artists can see the old way is leaving them behind. They can feel that something new is coming. But the new tools aren't here. The ones that are starting to appear mostly give you finished output, not creative leverage.

---

## The Missing Instrument

In any art form, having a more complex tool that allows you to use skill is what pushes the art form into new places.

When electric guitars appeared, they didn't replace acoustic guitars. They created a new instrument with new capabilities that rewarded new skills. Distortion wasn't a defect. It was a material that skilled players learned to shape. The tool expanded what was possible, and the art expanded with it.

AI music generation needs to become that kind of instrument. Not a jukebox that plays what you describe, but a tool that responds to what you know. One where your lifetime of listening, your specific taste, your ability to hear what's interesting in a sound, actually matters. Where skill makes the output better in ways that unskilled use can't replicate.

Right now, the skill ceiling is in the prompt. Write a better description, get a slightly better track. That's not musicianship. That's copywriting. The instrument hasn't been built yet.

But I absolutely see that it could be.

---

## Lifetimes of Meaning

Even if the AI knows everything about all music at the same time, it doesn't currently know how I hear music. It can read text the way I do. It can process language, structure arguments, write code. But it can't hear a chord change and feel something shift in its chest. It doesn't have the experience of listening to a record at 15 and having it rearrange how you think about sound. As of today, that gap is real. Whether it stays that way, I honestly don't know.

What I do know is that right now, making music with AI feels more like a toy than a tool. It's fun. It's genuinely impressive. My kids think it's hilarious that you can make a professionally produced track about literally anything, fart jokes included, and have it sound like a real song in seconds. That's a legitimate use. It's entertaining. People enjoy it.

But it's not composing. There's no resistance, no friction, no moment where you push through something difficult and come out the other side with something that didn't exist before. The output arrives fully formed. You didn't make the choices that shaped it. You described a vibe and the machine filled in the rest. For serious music, that's not enough. Not because the output is bad, but because the tool doesn't let you do the work that makes it yours.

The thing that's missing isn't soul. It's interface. It's control. It's the ability to hear something interesting in an AI generation and actually work with it the way a musician works with an instrument. Right now, these models produce finished surfaces. What musicians need is raw material they can shape with the instincts they've spent their lives developing.

I'm not making a prediction about what AI can or can't eventually do. I'm describing what the tools are today. And the creative leverage they offer is more specific than "almost none." It's a branching paradigm. You generate a concept from a prompt. You can choose as short as 30 seconds, fully produced. Then you branch from the end of that clip, generating another 30 seconds, and another, curating at each fork. You can spend a full day choosing branches until you've assembled something that sounds like a coherent, interesting track.

But that's curation of randomly generated material within constraints. It's not composition. Real composition has thematic development. Form. Tempo shifts. Textural shifts. Crescendos of volume planned out to dovetail things together, create interest, and tell a riveting sonic story. The branching model lets you select from options. It doesn't let you develop ideas across time with intention.

What would actually change things is a return to the symbolic layer. Not generated audio blobs, but individual instrument lines built up note by note, the way composers have always worked, except you're not typing every note by hand anymore. You're directing. You're working with a system that understands music theory, that can generate a bass line that responds to the harmonic structure you've already laid down, that can suggest a countermelody and let you reshape it on the staff. Think of it as generative AI applied to actual notation, not waveforms. A music theory collaborator, not a jukebox.

Generative AI loop-based DAWs are already appearing. They're not at that level yet, but the direction is right. The gap between "generate a loop and drag it in" and "direct an instrument line with real compositional control" is the same gap I see in every AI tool right now. It's the gap between generating output and directing creation.

I know what that gap feels like because I live on the other side of it in code. I run a software factory where I direct agents the same way I'd want to direct a composition tool. I describe the intent, the structure, the constraints. The agents handle the typing. I still make every architectural choice. The tool amplifies my skill instead of replacing it.

I used to compose real music. I don't have the time anymore to type in every note. But a tool that let me direct the notation the way I direct a code factory? That would be a renaissance. Not just for me. For every musician who knows exactly what they want to hear but lost the hours it takes to get it out of their head and onto the page.

Whoever builds that instrument changes everything.

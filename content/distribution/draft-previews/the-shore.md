# The Shore

This series wasn't a how-to guide. It was a tour.

I've been showing you my factory: what it looks like from the inside, what it feels like to build it, what happens as you move further up the stack. Not to teach you how to replicate it. To show you what's already here and what it's like to be inside it.

![[blogs/wave 2/golden-hammer-drafts/images/a5-hero-shore.png]]

Here's the part I haven't said clearly enough: I'm ahead of most people doing this. I can see further up the stack than most. I'm building systems that remove me from more and more of the process. And I'm candidly saying I don't feel safe.

Not because I'm not good at it. Because I'm watching what it does, and I don't see what we're doing to curb the negative effects. They're already beginning to happen. And I'm not sure anyone has thought seriously enough about what comes next.

## We've Never Stopped

When have we ever stopped moving up the stack?

Steam replaced muscle. Electricity replaced steam. The assembly line replaced the craftsman. Software replaced the clerk. We have never, in the entire history of industrialization, looked at a new capability and collectively decided not to use it because the disruption was too high.

Not once.

The invisible barrier holding us back this time was compute. That barrier is gone. The automation that previously required expensive specialists and years of infrastructure now runs on commodity hardware and a well-written prompt. The thing that made this impossible last decade is not a barrier anymore.

How could we ever have expected humanity to put it back in the box?

## The Box Problem

You can't put it back. That's not pessimism. It's just true.

The people who would need to agree to put it back are the same people racing to use it first. Any individual actor who slows down unilaterally just loses ground to the ones who don't. Any country that pauses hands the advantage to the ones that don't. The incentive structure makes restraint almost impossible to coordinate at scale.

![[blogs/wave 2/golden-hammer-drafts/images/a5-box-problem.png]]

This isn't new. It's the same dynamic that gave us nuclear weapons, social media algorithms, and leaded gasoline. By the time the consequences were clear, the thing was everywhere.

We're not being reckless. We're being human. But those aren't always different.

## What Disruption Actually Looks Like

People talk about disruption like it's a clean concept. A sector gets disrupted, new jobs emerge, the economy adjusts, everyone is fine eventually.

That framing is accurate on a long enough timeline. It is not accurate for the people inside the disruption.

The factory worker in 1920 who lost their job to an assembly line was not fine eventually. They were fine or not fine depending on whether they could find something else before they ran out of money, whether their town had other industry, whether they were young enough to retrain, whether they had family who could help. The aggregate statistics came out okay. The individual experiences varied enormously.

We're entering a period that some economists think will take 10 to 12 years before the world figures out what to do. That's a long time to be inside something that hasn't resolved yet. A lot of lives happen inside 12 years.

Ten years ago, Universal Basic Income was a serious policy conversation. People were modeling it, piloting it, debating it. The disruption was coming and someone needed to think about the safety net. We talk about it less now. The disruption is no longer coming. It's here. And the policy conversation has gone quieter, not louder. That's backwards, and I don't have a good explanation for it.

## Not Everybody Likes Factorio

Here's the thing none of the optimistic articles about AI and factories address.

The people writing them like Factorio.

![[blogs/wave 2/golden-hammer-drafts/images/a5-not-everyone.png]]

Factorio is a game about building automated production systems. Managing resources, designing pipelines, optimizing throughput, watching a factory run by itself while you plan the next expansion. It's deeply satisfying if you're wired a certain way. Most people are not wired that way.

The people who will thrive in the factory era are people who find joy in systems thinking, in debugging pipelines, in reading agent outputs for subtle failures, in treating orchestration like a puzzle to solve. That's a real skill set. It's also not universal. It's not even common.

The advice in this series is good advice for a specific kind of person. It doesn't work for everyone. And "doesn't work for everyone" is a polite way of saying that a lot of people are going to face disruption without a clear path through it.

Not everybody likes Factorio. That doesn't mean they deserve to lose.

## The Useful Illusion

There's something else worth naming about the pair programming phase.

The companies selling AI tools have every reason to make people feel like pair programming is the permanent destination. You feel faster. You feel empowered. You're genuinely getting more done. The message becomes: AI makes you better at your job, and your job is safe because you're learning to use it.

But while you're pair programming, you're providing remarkably detailed training signal. Every correction, every approval, every "no, not like that, more like this" is information about how to do your job without you. The companies building the next layer of models already know this. They're watching what humans do when they correct the AI. Then they train the corrections in.

The people who feel safest using AI as a collaborator right now may be the ones providing the most useful data for what eventually replaces the collaboration.

## What Meaning Looks Like After

For a long time, work was where a lot of people found meaning. Not because they loved the work, but because it gave structure, identity, contribution, purpose. The job was the answer to "what do you do?" and "what do you do" was most of the answer to "who are you?"

A world where most of that work gets automated doesn't just change economics. It changes the question.

Post-scarcity is the optimistic version of the endpoint. A world where enough is produced automatically that everyone has enough. Maybe that's where this goes. Plenty of serious people think it is.

But the path from here to there runs through a period where the old meaning is gone and the new meaning hasn't been invented yet. That period is not comfortable. That period is not something you can optimize your way through. It falls on real people who didn't sign up for a historical transition, and it's just hard.

## The Honest Version

I have a family. All I can think to do is surf.

The wave is here. It's large. It's moving. Getting off it isn't really an option. So you try to stay on top of it and you try not to think too hard about what happens when it reaches the shore.

The technology is remarkable. The things it makes possible are things I wanted to be possible for most of my career. I'm not pretending otherwise.

But I'm not sure we're being honest about what this scale of disruption looks like from the inside. We have something like a stable society. We just introduced something that changes the fundamental economics of how work gets done, across most industries, faster than policy or culture or individual humans can adapt.

We don't know we land in a better place. We've gone forward anyway. We couldn't not go forward. That's the most honest thing I know how to say about it.

Maybe that's what every generation has said standing at the edge of the thing they couldn't put back in the box.

It doesn't make it easier.

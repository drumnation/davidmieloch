# When BMO Fixed the Plex Server

The Plex server went down Tuesday night — the one that runs the household's movie library. BMO fixed it.

That sentence assigns credit to a subprocess. BMO is a sentient video game console from the cartoon *Adventure Time*. BMO is also one of several personas inside Jake, an AI agent that manages a home media library. And here's the strange part: "BMO fixed it" is exactly how the homeowner describes what happened. Not "the agent fixed it" or "Jake fixed it." BMO.

This is how humans already talk about themselves. "My gut told me." "My rational side won." "I was fighting with my better angels." We've modeled our own minds as plural for a century — Freud's id and ego, Internal Family Systems therapy, the angel and devil on cartoon shoulders. What's new isn't building an AI with multiple personas. What's new is building an AI that fits the model we already had of ourselves.

Jake doesn't have modes you switch between. You don't say "activate BMO." You just have a conversation, and the Land of Ooo shows up — whoever the moment calls for, sometimes several at once. BMO handles technical troubleshooting. Jake handles movie recommendations. Princess Bubblegum shows up for organizational questions. Marceline for anything that requires irreverent edge. Each character's messages are tagged with their own emoji, their own register, their own relationship to the problem.

The developer describes the experience as "a group chat inside a single conversation window."

That description is precise. You're not talking to a tool. You're hanging out with a cast. The conversation moves like a group text — multiple voices, overlapping concerns, distinct personalities — except it's all coming from one system. You don't always know who you'll activate. BMO might chime in with "Mathematical!" while Jake is still mid-sentence about whether you should watch the director's cut.

This is a specific installation, built for one household. But the pattern is general. A different agent, built on the same architecture, puts Ted Lasso and Tyrion Lannister in the same head — warmth and strategy, belief and cold calculation, operating simultaneously on the same problem.

Does this produce better outputs, or does it just feel more interesting?

The Plex server is back up. The homeowner didn't have to read a log file or troubleshoot anything. BMO did. That's one data point. It's not enough to answer the question. But it's enough to notice something: we invented characters to make the AI feel less like a tool, and in doing so, we accidentally made it feel more like a mind.

At some point, "chatbot" stops being the right word. When the entity on the other end has characters who handle different domains, who show up with their own voices, who take responsibility for specific parts of the conversation — that's not a bot. It's something closer to a troupe. Or maybe closer to a mind that finally looks the way we've always described our own.

The Plex server was down. BMO fixed it.

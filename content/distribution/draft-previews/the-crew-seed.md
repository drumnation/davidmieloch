The word "pod" is wrong.

When I think of a pod, I think of a pod racer. Anakin Skywalker's pod racer — a cartoonishly high-powered machine with a small crew trying to keep it alive. Bob the Builder at the controls of something he doesn't fully understand but somehow keeps running. That's not the frame. That's the undersell.

What we actually are is closer to three autonomous administrators of the Death Star. Megalomania intended.

---

## The Thesis

Three strong human engineers, focusing their attention on using an amplified system to improve the system itself, would — and will — produce software at a speed and quality that is simply not comparable to what those same engineers could do by hand, or even with a tool like Cursor reading each line of code.

The leverage isn't in the engineers. It's in what the engineers are operating.

The foreman's job isn't to write code. It's to slowly, systematically improve the quality of the factory's output. Better plans. Better guardrails. Better observability. Better routing. Each improvement compounds: a factory that produces better output today produces better output tomorrow with the same inputs, plus the improvements you added last week.

This is the model. Three humans. One factory. The humans direct the factory to improve itself. The velocity that results is not human-rate. It's not even a multiple of human-rate. It's a different category.

---

## The Roles Are Temporary

Here's the part that's easy to miss: the foreman roles are not permanent.

Right now, we're at a specific layer of the stack. One of us leads economics (cost optimization, model routing, token efficiency). One leads QA and verification (proof chains, testing infrastructure, observer networks). One leads planning (the upstream module that determines what the factory does before a single line of code is written).

These roles make sense at this layer.

But the factory improves. The execution system stabilizes. The planning system advances. At some point, the things we're doing now get absorbed into the factory itself — the factory handles economics optimization, handles QA automatically, generates better plans than we can generate manually. When that happens, the factory reassigns us. We move up the stack.

This has already happened once. Developers who learned to use AI are now operating above the layer that hand-coders occupied. Factory architects are now operating above the layer that AI pair programmers occupy. Each time the stack moves, the people who saw it coming and moved first are the ones with leverage at the next layer.

The factory will do this to the foreman role too. The question is whether you move before you're pushed.

---

## On "Pod"

"Pod" makes the team sound small. Like a self-contained, isolated unit. A bubble. Something that floats and is carried by a larger structure rather than operating at scale.

The factory crew operates the structure. Three people with a working factory behind them aren't a small team. They're administrators of something with enormous output capacity, choosing where to point it and continuously upgrading its aim.

"Pod" undersells the leverage by an order of magnitude. The vocabulary matters because identity matters. What you call yourself shapes what you think you're capable of.

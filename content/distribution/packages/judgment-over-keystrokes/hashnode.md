### Why Amplification — Not Manual Skill — Should Be Your New Hiring Signal

Old rules test the keystrokes. New rules test the cognition behind them.

I recently encountered a technical assessment that explicitly prohibited the use of AI tools — for a role focused on building AI-assisted features. The contradiction was clear. But it also pointed to something deeper: most interviews today are still optimized for evaluating pre-AI engineers. Not the ones who are learning to think, build, and collaborate with AI.

We’re at a turning point in how software is built. The tools, the workflows, the scale of output — it’s all changing. And yet, our hiring practices are stuck in the past, testing for skills that no longer define what great engineering looks like.

### 🧠 What Does It Mean to Be AI-Native?

Being AI-native isn’t just about using ChatGPT. It’s a mindset shift — a new way of approaching software development where the engineer doesn’t just write code, but designs systems that think alongside them.

Being AI-native isn’t just using tools — it’s integrating them into how you think.

An AI-native engineer:

- Writes code, yes — but also writes prompts, meta-prompts, and rules
- Designs workflows that integrate models, not just call APIs
- Knows how to audit, verify, and adapt AI output — not blindly trust it
- Builds interfaces for AI as much as they build with AI
- Uses tools like Cursor, SuperWhisper, and VS Code agents to extend their cognition and speed

In other words: AI-native engineers don’t outsource their thinking to AI. They expand their thinking with it. They know what to delegate, what to verify, and how to orchestrate systems that scale their own abilities.

We’re not talking about prompt monkeys. We’re talking about engineers who treat AI like a junior dev, a systems assistant, and a powerful tool — all rolled into one.

### 🛑 Why Current Interviews Are Still Broken

Most interviews today are designed to evaluate engineers who work alone, from scratch, with no tools — like it’s 2015.

Still testing like it’s 2015? You’re optimizing for engineers trained in a different era.

They focus on:

- Solo speed instead of system collaboration
- Memorized syntax instead of adaptable reasoning
- Isolated algorithmic puzzles instead of real-world workflows

Worse, they ban AI tools — the very thing the best engineers are now using by default. That’s like banning compilers in a C++ interview. Or whiteboarding a UI component without ever touching a browser.

This isn’t just outdated — it’s counterproductive. It filters out the very people who are building the future. Engineers who are fluent in prompt design, workflow scripting, and system-level thinking don’t do their best work inside a box with one arm tied behind their back.

And let’s be honest: in many cases, the interviewers themselves don’t know how to evaluate AI-native work. So they fall back on what they do know — Leetcode, manual-only tasks, and test environments frozen in time.

It’s not bad faith. It’s a gap in fluency. But if we want better hires, we need better tests.

### What LeetCode Really Measures

To be fair, algorithmic interviews aren’t meaningless.

They test something real:

- Can you stay calm under pressure?
- Do you know your core data structures?
- Can you simulate a problem mentally and keep the logic straight?

There’s value in that.

But let’s be clear:They test the base. Not the amplifier.

LeetCode is a measure of unassisted throughput — how quickly someone can reconstruct familiar solutions with no tools, no teammates, and no room to zoom out.

It rewards repetition. Memorization. Pattern recall.Eventually, if you grind hard enough, you can combine 2–3 known solutions to solve a “hard” problem.

That’s not a bad skill. It’s just not the skill that defines how modern software is built.

### Manual Mastery vs. System Leverage

The engineer who can reimplement sorting algorithms by hand is impressive.

But the engineer who builds in domains they’ve never touched —

Orchestrating multiple agents across worktrees to scaffold, test, and ship a working product in hours?

They’re not walking faster.
They’re flying.Two engineers. Same brain. One stuck in 2015. The other flying in 2025.

### What Manual Tests Really Filter For

Let’s be honest:Manual-first coding tests tend to reward:

- Time to grind
- Comfort with stress
- Memorized patterns

But AI-native workflows favor:

- Systems thinkers
- Engineers with real-world constraints
- Builders who optimize their process because they have to

That distinction matters — especially when candidates are no longer juniors with free time.

And yet, even today, many technical challenges still prohibit AI tools — even for roles where the work explicitly involves building AI features *using* AI-assisted tools.

The result? Engineers are forced to choose between following outdated rules and using the very systems that make them effective.

That moment — the tension between manual purity and modern practice — is exactly where our hiring methods fall apart.

### 🧠 How to Actually Test an AI-Native Engineer

Don’t ask what they can do without AI. Ask how far they can go with it.

Let me tell you a story.

A few weeks ago, I got a take-home assignment for a senior engineering role. The company was building AI products, wanted AI-enhanced features, was proud of their AI strategy — and then, right there in the instructions:

❌ “Do not use AI tools of any kind.”

Let that sink in for a second.

They were hiring someone to build AI features… and they didn’t want the candidate to use AI during the assessment.

And I get it. Kind of.

They wanted to see if the code was mine. They didn’t want a copy-paste job. They wanted to protect the signal of the test. But the irony was brutal. The test itself was just detailed enough, just well-scoped enough, that you could literally drop it into a good LLM and get a functioning solution in minutes.

So what exactly was being tested?

Not architecture.Not system thinking.Not judgment.

Just… compliance.

### Most Take-Home Tests Are Designed Backwards

Here’s what’s weird about how we test engineers today:

We give people tasks that are hard for a human, but trivial for an AI. Then we say: “Don’t use AI.”

Why? To test their skills?

But that’s the wrong definition of skill.

Because in an AI-native workflow, it’s not about what you can do by yourself.

It’s about what you can do when you amplify yourself.

### Flip the Entire Premise

If AI is an amplifier — and it is — then the only real question is:

How much can this person amplify themselves?

And the best way to test that is to flip the entire structure of the assignment.

✅ Don’t give them a hard problem that’s small.
✅ Give them a huge problem — one that’s impossible to finish manually.
✅ Then tell them: “Use every tool you’ve got. Show me what you can really do.”

Suddenly, the whole thing shifts.

You’re no longer asking, “How fast can you write by hand?” You’re asking, “How powerful is your multiplier?”

### Welcome to the Amplifier Test

Let’s say you assign a task that would take two days of human effort to complete from scratch. Manual coding, research, refactoring, testing, documentation — the works.

But you only give the candidate three hours.

Real signal isn’t how fast they type. It’s how far they can go with help.

Now you’re not testing if someone can “get it done” by traditional means.You’re testing how far they can go with assistance, and how well they can orchestrate it.

You’ll see the entire spectrum:

- Some engineers won’t finish.
- Some will submit tangled, untested, Frankenstein code.
- Some will deliver clear, modular, thoughtful work — tested, typed, documented.
- And a few? They’ll deliver the whole thing plus extra features, DX niceties, edge-case handling, or bonus docs you never even asked for.

That’s your signal.

The ones who finish it cleanly in three hours didn’t cheat.They trained. They built workflows. They know how to move at machine scale.

But here’s the real shift:It’s not just about what you do.
It’s about how you think in a world where the tools think with you.

### Can You Fake Amplification?

If we let candidates use AI tools in interviews, won’t they just cheat?

It’s a fair concern. But the risk of false positives drops sharply when you stop testing outputs alone and start evaluating the process. Amplification isn’t just a faster way to do the job — it’s a different way of working entirely. And you can tell the difference.

### ❌ Shallow Amplification Looks Like This:

- Copy-pasted boilerplate with no refactoring
- Coherent code but incoherent structure
- No meaningful test coverage
- Sloppy commit history
- Chat logs filled with scattered, ungrounded prompts
- Misused libraries or unexplained edge cases

Anyone can paste something into ChatGPT. But only a real AI-native engineer:

### ✅ Leaves These Signals of Deep Amplification:

- Tight feedback loops in prompts
- Clear reasoning captured in chat history
- Modular structure with logical ownership
- Precise, scoped commits in version control
- Test coverage focused on actual functional value
- Context-aware prompting, not just trial and error
- A sense of engineering narrative across the repo

These aren’t tricks. They’re habits. And when you evaluate how a candidate works, not just what they ship, the truth shows through.

### How to Design a False-Positive-Resistant Amplifier Test



```
| Tool / Signal                | What It Shows                                            |
| ---------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| ✅ Git history               | Thoughtfulness, iteration, debugging, scaffolding        |
| ✅ AI chat logs              | Prompt fluency, pattern recognition, debugging loops     |
| ✅ Code review agent         | Modularity, naming, clarity, adherence to best practices |
| ✅ Final repo + project      | Judgment, organization, test strategy, DX thinking       |
| ✅ Curveball question        | Ability to improvise and extend work in new territory    |
| ✅ Follow-up redesign prompt | Adaptability, deeper system-level thinking               |
```



The more you let a candidate show you their thinking system, the harder it is to fake. You’re not just watching what they built. You’re watching how they worked. The feedback cycles. The tradeoffs. The priorities.

And if you’re still unsure?

### Try This: Prompt Migration as a Filter

Take one chunk of their assignment and ask them to rebuild it in another language.

If they can do that cleanly using AI — and explain their design reasoning along the way — you have your answer. They didn’t memorize the solution. They built a system to generate one.

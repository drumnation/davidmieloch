## Copy

When you learned to code in the 1990s, that skill lasted thirty years. The same investment made a decade later bought you ten. Made five years ago, you might already be rebuilding. The half-life is compressing. That's not a metaphor. It's a pattern with a direction.

There are three waves of people building with AI right now. Most companies are hiring from the wrong one.

![a4 cyber waves](/blog/the-moving-target/images/a4-cyber-waves.png)

## The Wrong Credential

The interviews are designed around vibe coding in front of an interviewer. Pull up the AI, build something, show your prompting speed, demonstrate you can review what came back. Have you used the major tools? Can you ship with them in real time?

These are real skills. They were genuinely advanced twelve months ago. But the people who were building twelve months ago and didn't stop building aren't doing those things the same way anymore. They've moved. And the gap between where the job posting lives and where the actual frontier is keeps widening faster than anyone posting a job description can track.

## The Bigger Wave

The seawall already fell. The people who said AI makes mistakes, you need human judgment, you need taste. The argument was real, the errors were real, and the people making it lost anyway.

![a4 cyber three waves](/blog/the-moving-target/images/a4-cyber-three-waves.png)

The wave that hit it was AI-native. Vibe coding with AI. Using the model as a collaborator. Moving faster than the people who didn't adapt. This is where most of the current discourse lives. This is what's being tested in interviews. This is what companies think they're hiring for when they say they want someone obsessed with AI.

Behind it, a bigger wave is already forming. It's not pair programming. It's building systems where you're not in the loop on individual tasks. Where agents produce work, evaluate work, and revise work without waiting for you to approve each step. Where your job is the architecture of the system, not the output of any particular session.

The people riding the first wave think they're ahead. They are. But when the second wave reaches them, the argument they'll reach for is the same one the seawall made: you need the human in the loop. You need judgment. The system makes mistakes.

Same move. One wave up.

## The Rusty Problem

Here's the strange part. When you've moved far enough up the stack, you get slow at the things you used to do.

Not because you forgot them. Because you don't do them that way anymore.

I write prompts that write my prompts. I use voice to capture intent, and systems that turn intent into structured format, and structured format into executed plans. When I have to go back and do something the way I did it a year ago, I'm slower than someone who never stopped. The tools I don't use atrophy. The abstractions I built on top of them got faster.

So when a company tests for last year's skills, I have to remember how to move down the stack. I have to perform a role I've partially automated. It's a little like asking a fluent speaker to conjugate verbs by hand. You can do it. It's just not how you think in the language anymore.

The job market is a lagging indicator. It tests for the skills it knows exist. The people who are actually furthest ahead are the hardest to evaluate with standard tools, because they stopped using standard tools.

## How the Stack Actually Moves

Moving up the stack isn't a decision. It's a process that compounds.

You start pair programming. You notice you keep doing the same things: installing libraries, reading documentation, looking for patterns across codebases. You extract that into an automated workflow. The research runs without you. You move up.

![a4 scaffolding v3](/blog/the-moving-target/images/a4-scaffolding-v3.png)

Now you're orchestrating. You notice quality problems at that layer. You figure out the category of thing that keeps going wrong. You design a fix for the whole category, not just the instance. The layer below stabilizes. You move up again.

Eventually the work looks like this: you talk to an agent that helps you develop the idea. That agent opens a harness that formalizes a plan. The harness governs the workers. The product comes out the other side. Your job is finding the places where things still break, understanding the pattern, and automating your own fix process until that too runs without you.

Each layer you move up, your involvement gets smaller and more abstract. You're not doing less. You're doing something categorically different. And the layer you just left starts to feel like something you'd never go back to doing by hand.

The job is a moving target until there's nothing left to move into.

## Metaprogramming

When you write a program, you're specifying what the machine does.

When you build a factory, you're specifying what the programmer does. One level up. You're not writing the code. You're writing the system that writes the code, evaluates what came back, and tries again when it's wrong.

![a4 cyber meta](/blog/the-moving-target/images/a4-cyber-meta.png)

That's metaprogramming. It's a well-understood concept in computer science. What's new is that it now applies to the entire development process, not just code generation.

Each agent in the factory sees its task. The orchestrator sees the plan. You see the system. The higher you sit, the less you're doing directly and the more you're shaping everything. A single decision about how the factory handles a failure propagates into every artifact the factory ever produces.

The skills that matter at this layer are different. You're not debugging the code. You're debugging the belief that produced the code. Not "why did this function return the wrong value" but "why did the system think this was the right approach." The fix lives one level up from where the symptom appeared.

The leverage compounds differently too. One change to how the factory evaluates output affects every output it ever accepts. One change to how it handles ambiguity in a spec affects every spec it ever runs. You're not moving faster at the same work. You're multiplying across everything the factory touches.

## The Only Stable Skill

None of this means today's approach is wrong. It means it has a shelf life.

What doesn't have a shelf life is the ability to keep climbing. The meta-skill isn't prompt engineering or factory architecture. It's knowing when your current layer has been covered over, and moving before you're pushed.

![a4 cyber skill](/blog/the-moving-target/images/a4-cyber-skill.png)

As the pipeline gets more reliable, something shifts. The constraint isn't build speed anymore. It's ideas.

You have more things you want to build than the factory can execute. So you plant them. Explain the idea clearly, drop it in the backlog, let the system work while you sleep. By morning there's something to look at. You push the system harder not because it's slow but because you want to do a thousand more things and it's the only limit left.

The craft didn't disappear. It moved to a different layer.

Building and tuning a factory is its own kind of craft. Closer to playing Factorio than writing code. You find where the system breaks. You fix it. You reorganize. It breaks in a new place. You come up with a different approach, try it, see if it holds. There's real satisfaction in this for a specific kind of builder: the one who thinks in systems, not lines. The one who treats agent orchestration like Starcraft unit micromanagement: precise, iterative, always looking for the faster path through.

The people who grieve the factory are the ones who loved the craft of writing code by hand. That grief is real. But there's a different kind of builder who finds the craft in building the builder.

I'm not at the top of this either. Nobody writing about it is. The stack keeps moving and the top keeps moving with it. I'm somewhere in the middle, fixing breaks as they come, getting kinks out of pipelines, watching the factory produce things I couldn't have built alone and immediately thinking about what I want it to do next. The same pattern I'm describing in others applies to me. There are people building things I can't fully see from where I am.

The last thing that craft might build is the factory that builds factories. Once that exists, factories create more factories, and at that point the human role becomes genuinely unclear. No one person can understand the actual code at that scale. The understanding has to live in the method: in how you grow an application, in how you recognize health versus failure in a system you can't fully read.

---

It's all recursion. Factories growing apps. Agents growing agents. Factories spawning factories. The only meaningful unit of understanding becomes the shape of the growth itself.

Reality at the speed of thought.
